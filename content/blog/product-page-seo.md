A product page has two jobs. It has to show up in search, and it has to convince the person who clicks. Most stores focus on one and neglect the other. The page ranks for a search, then loses the buyer because the copy reads like a catalog entry.

This guide covers the parts of a product page that matter for both, and how to write them.

## Start with the search, then the buyer

Before writing anything, find out how people search for the product. Look at the words they use, not the words in your catalog. Shoppers say "wedge for beginners" or "foam cannon for boats," even when the product is named something more technical.

Once you know the search, check what the buyer needs to know before they'll buy. That might be sizing, compatibility, care instructions or how it compares with the cheaper option. The page has to answer both sets of questions.

## The page title

The title tag is what appears in search results. It should name the product clearly and include the main search term once, near the start. Keep it under about 60 characters so it isn't cut off.

Weak: "Wedge 58 | Shop Now | Our Store"

Stronger: "58° Wedge for Short Pitches and Bunker Shots"

The stronger version names the product, states its use and matches how people search. It also reads like a headline, which is what gets the click.

## The meta description

The description doesn't directly affect rankings, but it affects clicks. Use it to say what the buyer gets, in one or two sentences. Avoid stuffing keywords, and don't repeat the title word for word.

## The opening copy

The first two sentences of the product copy should answer the most common question about the product. They should use the search terms naturally, without forcing them. If a shopper lands on the page from a search, those opening lines should match what they were looking for.

## Headings and structure

Use one main heading on the page, which matches the product name. Use sub-headings for the things buyers look for: features, sizing, compatibility, care and delivery. Headings help people scan the page, and they help search engines understand its structure.

## Product details

Put the specifications in a clear list, not buried in a paragraph. Include the details a buyer would search for: materials, dimensions, compatible models and what's included. Each detail is a possible search term, and it helps a buyer decide.

## Images and alt text

Every product image should have a short alt text that describes what the image shows. Alt text helps visually impaired shoppers, and it helps search engines understand the page. Keep it specific: "58 degree wedge with a steel head" is better than "wedge."

## Reviews and questions

Customer reviews and questions add fresh, specific language to a page, and they often include the exact words buyers use. Show them on the page where they can be read, and answer the questions openly.

## What to avoid

- Copy copied from the manufacturer, which many other stores also use.
- Duplicate titles and descriptions across similar products.
- Keyword stuffing, which reads badly and can cause search engines to ignore the page.
- Thin pages with a photo, a price and two lines of text.

## Where to start

Pick your ten best-selling products, check how they appear in search, and rewrite the titles and opening copy first. Measure the change in visits and sales over the next few weeks before moving on. That approach shows you which changes matter for your store.

Many ecommerce SEO audits look impressive. They run to dozens of pages, list hundreds of warnings and end with a score. Then the store owner asks what to fix first, and the answer is unclear.

A useful audit does something different. It finds the problems that cost the most, explains why, and puts them in an order you can act on.

## What a good audit covers

### 1. Crawling and indexing

Can Google reach the pages you want it to find, and is it keeping the right ones? Look for orphaned products, redirect chains, pages blocked by mistake, and pages that are indexed but shouldn't be, such as filter combinations.

### 2. Site speed and mobile experience

Most ecommerce traffic arrives on phones. Test real pages on mobile and desktop, and look at the heaviest problems: large images, slow scripts and apps that load on every page. Speed affects both rankings and whether shoppers stay.

### 3. On-page structure

Check titles, descriptions and headings on the most important pages. Look for duplicates, missing descriptions and pages with no clear main heading. The question is whether each page has one clear job.

### 4. Product and collection content

Review the copy on your top products and collections. Is it specific, or copied from the manufacturer? Does it answer the questions a buyer would ask? Thin collection pages are a common and costly gap.

### 5. Search demand and keyword fit

Compare the pages you have with the searches people actually make. Some valuable searches may have no page at all, and some pages may target searches nobody types. This is where the audit connects to revenue.

### 6. Links

Look at who links to the store, and where the links come from. Identify links from low-quality or unrelated sites, and check whether any of them are harmful enough to need removing.

### 7. Structured data and search features

Check whether products, reviews and FAQs are marked up correctly. Structured data helps search engines understand the page, and it can make results more visible.

### 8. Measurement

Confirm that analytics and Search Console are set up correctly, and that you can see visits and revenue from search. An audit without measurement can't prove that the fixes worked.

## What a useful audit looks like

A good audit does four things:

1. **It ranks the problems by impact.** A broken collection page that drives sales matters more than a long meta description on a blog post nobody reads.
2. **It explains each problem in plain language.** You should understand what's wrong and why it matters, without a glossary.
3. **It gives an order of work.** You should know what to fix this week, this month and later.
4. **It says what it can't tell you.** Honest audits note where the data is limited, and where a test is needed.

## How to tell a weak audit from a strong one

A weak audit is a long list of warnings with no order, no revenue link and no explanation. Look for a score that means nothing, and for fixes that are generic and could apply to any site.

A strong audit is shorter, and it's specific to your store. It names your products, your collections and your buyers. It tells you what to do first, and why.

## What to ask for

Ask for the top ten problems, ranked by expected impact, with a short explanation of each. Ask how each fix will be checked afterwards. And ask what the audit doesn't cover, so you know where the gaps are.
